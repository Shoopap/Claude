NeuroLockdown

10:47 PM. Tomorrow is Maya's big science test. Your class has been shrunk to the size of a single cell and sent into her brain to help her study. The moment you arrive, everything goes into LOCKDOWN. Unlock the Brain Box before morning.

How it works

  • Six paper stations around the room, one per brain region. Each has field notes (the science) and a hands-on puzzle. Solving it gives the code to one lock on the Brain Box.
  • One Brain Terminal, a laptop or Chromebook running the game in Classroom mode. It shows the countdown and holds the two rooms that need sound and timing: the Amygdala and the Temporal lobes. The terminal gives the code for the Synapse Lab pouch.
  • The Brain Box has six locks. Inside is the Frontal lobe finale: letter tiles to unscramble, a very tempting red envelope, and the final override, typed into the terminal.

At a glance

Players6–10 per kit (the whole team works at once, splitting across stations)
Time30 or 45 minutes, plus 5 min briefing and 5 min debrief
Tech1 laptop/Chromebook with sound. Open index.html, choose Classroom.
CoversAll 12 primary and 6 supporting terms from the project
CostAbout $35–50 for locks (reusable), or $0 with the envelope option

The flow

Stations 1–5Paper puzzles, any order, all at once. Each opens one lock on the Brain Box.
Brain TerminalCalm the Amygdala → tune the Sound Lab → get the Synapse pouch code.
Station 6: SynapseMaterials are in the locked pouch. Solving it reveals where the Brain Box key is hidden.
Brain Box → Frontal lobeAll six locks open → unscramble the tiles → type the override at the terminal. Escape!

Why hybrid?

Players physically handle the science: they wire yarn through a thalamus, carry a code across the room in their memory, snap neurotransmitters into receptors and argue over which structures belong in the limbic system. The laptop is used only where tech does something paper can't: a live stress alarm that responds to breathing, and a radio you have to tune by ear.

Running the game

  • Give 3 free hints per team. After that, each hint costs 1 minute (subtract it on the board).
  • If a team opens the red envelope, add 1 minute. The card inside explains why.
  • The terminal's own hints cost 30 seconds and are tracked on screen.
  • Don't give answers. Point back to the field notes. Every answer is in them.

Debrief questions (5 min)

  1. Which station made you use your frontal lobe the most? Why?
  2. When the clock got low, what did your amygdala do? What helped?
  3. What memory strategy did your team use at the Memory Vault?
  4. Name one habit from today that would help your brain before a test.

BrainstemLife Support

OPENS4-digit locktagged BRAINSTEM

Alarms everywhere. Maya's breathing and heartbeat are stuttering. The brain's autopilot needs the right supplies, and fast.

The brainstem is the oldest part of your brain. It sits at the base of your skull and connects your brain to your spinal cord.

What it runs, automatically

  • Breathing
  • Heartbeat and blood pressure
  • Swallowing, coughing and sneezing
  • Waking up and staying alert

It has three parts: the midbrain, the pons and the medulla. You never have to remember to breathe, because the brainstem does it for you, even while you sleep.

Supply report

Oxygen
Your brain is only about 2% of your body weight, but it uses about 20% of the oxygen you breathe.
Glucose
Glucose is a simple sugar from food, carried in your blood. It is the brain's main fuel. Neurons can't store much of it, so they need a steady supply. That's why skipping breakfast can make it hard to focus.
Water
Brain tissue is about 75% water. Even mild dehydration can make it harder to pay attention and remember.

ThalamusThe Relay Station

OPENSDirectional locktagged THALAMUS

Signals from Maya's senses are piling up in the middle of her brain with nowhere to go. Somebody has to run the switchboard.

The thalamus is a pair of egg-shaped structures right in the middle of your brain. It is the brain's relay station, like a switchboard operator.

  • Almost every signal from your senses stops at the thalamus first. It sorts the signal and sends it on to the right part of the brain's outer layer, the cortex.
  • It also relays movement signals and helps control how awake and alert you feel.

Where signals go

Occipital lobe
At the back of your head. Handles vision.
Parietal lobe
On top, behind the frontal lobe. Handles touch, pain, temperature and body position.
Temporal lobes
On the sides, by your ears. Handle hearing.
Frontal lobe
At the front. Its motor cortex turns movement plans into action.

The one exception

Smell skips the thalamus. Smell signals travel from your nose to the olfactory bulb, then straight to areas wired to the amygdala and hippocampus. That's why a smell can bring back a memory or feeling instantly.

Your mission

On the switchboard, run each sense's yarn through the thalamus hole in the middle, then to the part of the brain that handles it.

Your code is the direction each wire leaves the thalamus (→ ↑ ↓ ←), in sense-number order, 1 to 5. A signal that doesn't pass through the thalamus adds nothing to the code.

HypothalamusThe Thermostat

OPENS5-letter word locktagged HYPOTHALAMUS

Every body system is out of balance, and the alerts keep coming. The control tower needs an operator.

The hypothalamus is about the size of an almond and sits just below the thalamus. Hypo means "below." It is your body's thermostat and control tower.

Its big job is homeostasis: keeping your body balanced, no matter what's happening outside.

How it restores balance

Temperature
Too hot? It makes you sweat so you cool off. Too cold? It makes you shiver so your muscles make heat.
Water
When you're low on water and your blood gets too salty, it makes you feel thirsty.
Food
When blood glucose drops, it switches on hunger.
Sleep
Its built-in body clock makes you sleepy when it gets dark at night.
Stress
When the amygdala senses danger, the hypothalamus signals glands to release stress hormones like cortisol and adrenaline.

HippocampusThe Memory Vault

OPENS4-digit locktagged HIPPOCAMPUS

Tonight's studying is about to be lost. The code is stored in the Memory Vault on the far wall, and you can only carry it back in your head.

Hippocampus is Greek for seahorse, because it's curled like one. You have two, one inside each temporal lobe.

  • It turns short-term memories into long-term memories.
  • It helps you remember facts and events from your life.
  • It helps you find your way, like remembering the route to a new classroom.

Memory boosters

1. Sleep
While you sleep, the hippocampus replays what you learned that day and helps store it for good.
2. Practice
Quizzing yourself builds stronger memory than rereading notes.
3. Water
A hydrated, well-fed brain remembers better.
4. Calm
Stress that lasts a long time floods the brain with cortisol, which can harm the hippocampus.

Memory tricks

Chunking: group numbers into pairs, like 38-16. Rehearsal: repeat it out loud all the way back. Teamwork: each person remembers one piece.

Your mission

Find the Memory Vault poster somewhere in the room. The code is the numbers next to the four memory boosters from these notes, in the order they're listed.

Vault rules: No paper, pencils, phones or photos at the vault. Only one person at the vault at a time. Carry the numbers back in your memory. Switch runners as often as you like.

Limbic systemEmotion Headquarters

OPENS3-digit locktagged LIMBIC

Maya's emotion and memory team has been scattered all over the brain. Reassemble the team, and only the team.

The limbic system is a team of structures deep in the middle of the brain that work together on emotion, memory and motivation.

Limbus is Latin for "edge" or "border." The team forms a curved ring around the brain's core.

The team

Amygdala
Emotions, especially fear. The brain's alarm.
Hippocampus
Forms new memories.
Hypothalamus
Connects emotions to the body: heart rate, hormones, hunger.
Cingulate gyrus
A curved belt of cortex above the brain's center. Helps you notice and manage emotions and keep your attention on a task.

Many scientists also count the thalamus as a partner of the team.

Why it matters at school

Emotion and memory are wired together. You remember things better when you care about them, which is why a fun or surprising lesson sticks.

NeurotransmittersThe Synapse Lab

REVEALSWhere the key isfor the KEY lock

Maya's neurons have stopped talking to each other. Load the right chemical messenger into each synapse.

Your brain has about 86 billion neurons. They talk using electricity and chemistry.

  • A signal zips down a neuron as an electrical pulse.
  • At the end it reaches a tiny gap called a synapse.
  • Tiny bubbles called vesicles release neurotransmitters, chemical messengers that cross the gap and fit into receptors on the next neuron, like a key in a lock.

The messengers

Dopamine
Reward and motivation. Released when you reach a goal or expect something good. Makes you want to do it again. Also helps control smooth movement.
Serotonin
Mood, sleep and appetite. Helps you feel steady and calm. About 90% of your body's serotonin is made in your gut!
GABA
The brain's brakes. Tells neurons to slow down and fire less, which helps you feel calm and fall asleep.

Frontal lobeCommand Center · inside the Brain Box

FINALOverride codetyped at the terminal

The frontal lobe sits right behind your forehead. It's the largest lobe and the brain's command center.

  • Planning, organizing and making decisions
  • Focus and solving problems
  • Impulse control: stopping to think before you act
  • Personality and behavior
  • Movement (the motor cortex) and speaking (Broca's area)

Still under construction

The very front part, the prefrontal cortex, keeps developing until about age 25. Every time you practice planning, focusing or pausing before you react, you strengthen those connections.

Connections

When the amygdala's alarm is too loud, the frontal lobe struggles. Mindfulness, sleep, water and glucose all help it work at full power.
